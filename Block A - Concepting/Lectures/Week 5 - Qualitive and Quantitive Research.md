## What is the purpose of a research question?
> ""The scientist is not a person who gives the right answer, they are the one who asks the right questions.""

The way you formulate the questions directs the answer. It determines what you do later on. 

It should tell you what your study aims to answer after you have analyzed and interpreted your data.
	It should indicate the different variables / parts, the study aims address.

A research question should aim to enhance our understanding of the world or something.

## What should a good research question look like?
It should be big enough to allow us to understand something meaningfully, but small enough to allow us explore / investigate it
	It should be measurable - something you can operationalize.

## What should you consider when constructing your research question?
- What is already known? What does literature say?
- Can I actually answer this question? Are my variables measurable?
- Is it worth addressing? What is the relevance / contribution
- Can di do this realistically? Time? Resources? Is it feasible?
- Is it focused enough? Is my question too broad?
- Is it ethical? Ethics.

#### [[Quantitive]] research:
- Usually works with numerical data
- Measures amounts, frequencies or counts
- Compares groups or conditions
- Examines relationships between variables
- How much? How many? How often?
- Is X related to Y? Do groups differ?

#### [[Qualitative]] research:
- Usually works with non-numerical data
- Explores experiences, perceptions and meanings
- Can explore differences between peoples' accounts / experiences
- Can examine how people describe, interpret or understand something
- How is something experienced
- How do people experience or make sense of X?

#### Research practices to avoid
> What if your results don't support what you expected?

**Don't:**
- Change your research question simply to fit the results.
- Keep trying different analyses until something "interesting" or significant appears
- Report only to analyses that "worked"
These practices can increase the risk of false-positives, lead to unreliable findings, and have contributed to concerns about the research preregistration of research of the past

**Instead** be transparent:
- Preregistration = there are pushes towards documenting your research questions / hypotheses, methods and planned analyses before analyzing the data
- Unexpected or non-significant findings are still findings.

# Research tips
## The research process
Develop an operational research question, informed by literature   -   What did you do? Why?
Design your methods and run your study = data collections -     How did you do it?
Analyze your data = results   -   What did you find?
Research and interpret your findings   -   What does this mean?

> All you really need to know for the moment is that research is a lot more complicated than you might think, even if you start from a position of thinking it's pretty damn complicated in the first place.

Moving from an idea to a research question
Focused / clear, researchable / operationalizable, feasible, relevant, informed by literature, ethical
Area of interest --> preliminary research --> what is known? --> what still needs to be known? --> narrow the focus --> provisional RQ --> evaluate and refine

Step 1: Start with an area of interest and do preliminary research
1. Start with preliminary reading around the topic of your interest: what has already been studied, with whom, and how?
2. Make notes of what do we already know, and what do we still need to know?
3. Look for a useful direction: a gap, contradiction, limitation, new context, different population, or a question worth revisiting. Always worth looking at the discussion section of papers for avenues for future research.
The literature is not just for your literature review, it helps you decide what your subject could become.

Step 2: Turn the broad topic into possible questions
- You cannot examine everything - start to make choices in relation to your area of interest.
- Use your preliminary reading to decide on which part of your topic you want to examine.
	- What am I trying to understand?
	- Which aspects am I focusing on?
	- Who or what am I studying?
	- Is it realistic to investigate?

Step 3: Frame, evaluate and refine your question
Once you have a provisional RQ, test it.

## Research Questions
### Theoretical & Conceptual Question
An overview you can construct in how things work together. A question that seeks to explore ideas, concepts or frameworks to understand social behaviors, systems, or theories.
### Descriptive Question
A question that aims to describe a phenomenon, situation, or population as it currently exists.
### Exploratory Question
A question that seeks to explore new areas where little information is available. What is possible in this space? What are the options that exist?
### Explanatory Question
Explaining how a thing operates/functions. A question that looks for reasons or causes behind a certain phenomenon or event. Why does it work? Why should this be used?
### Methodological Question
How do we measure this? A question focused on the best way to conduct research, collect data, or solve a problem.
### Design and Development Question
A question focused on creating or improving a product, system, or process. 
### Performance Testing Question
A question that assesses the performance, reliability, or effectiveness of a system, product, or process. 
### Validation Questions
A question that aims to test whether something works as expected or whether an idea is accurate. We never measured this and just assumed. We have measure this, and are trying to reproduce the methodology to see if it works.
### Predictive Question
A question that tries to forecast future events or outcomes based on current trends and data. Confidence is a statistics. The margin of error depends on the field you're in (critical systems, life dependent). Can we predict with 95% accuracy that it will happen?
>Exception example: 95% of a successful medical operation is not great odds, creating concerns in the clients.
### Evaluative Question
A question that assesses the success, impact, or effectiveness of a policy, program, or idea.
Commonly an A/B study. 
### Normative Question
A question that involves value judgements or asks what "ought" to be done, focusing on ideals, standards, or ethical concerns. A minority study.
## Research Approach
Identify the level your research question is in and what type of question it is. This will tell you what kind of method you need.
